The Core Shift

Predicting a Category Instead of a Number

Regression answers "how much?" Classification answers "which one?" -- spam or not spam, churn or stay, malignant or benign, cat or dog or bird.

RegressionClassification
PredictsA numberA category (a "class")
ExampleHouse price: $410,000Email: spam
"Wrong" meansFar from the true numberPicked the wrong category entirely
Evaluated withMSE, RMSE, R²Accuracy, precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC (today)

The workflow doesn't change -- split data, engineer features, train, cross-validate, tune. Only the target's shape and the way you judge "good" change, and judging "good" turns out to need a lot more care than a single number like R² ever did.

In [ 4 ]:
How Many Categories?

Binary vs. Multiclass vs. Multilabel

Binary

Exactly two categories: churn / stay, spam / not spam, fraud / legitimate. The simplest and most common case -- most of today's tools are explained here first.

Multiclass

Three or more categories, but each row belongs to exactly one: species of flower, digit 0-9, product category.

Multilabel

A row can belong to several categories at once: a news article tagged both "politics" and "economy." Rarer, and out of scope for today.

Today's core ideas (confusion matrix, precision, recall) are built for binary classification first -- Part late in the lecture shows exactly how they extend to multiclass, since the extension is simple once binary is solid.

Nothing New Here, on Purpose

The Classification Workflow

Exactly Lecture 01's pipeline, unchanged: collect and clean data, split into train/test, train a model, evaluate, tune, deploy. Every notebook so far has practiced this shape -- today just points it at a categorical target instead of a numeric one.

What genuinely differs starting today: the "Evaluate" step. A single number like R² doesn't have an equivalent that's always right for classification -- which metric is "right" depends on the problem, and picking badly can make a bad model look great. That's most of this lecture.

In [ 6 ]:
A Simple First Classifier

K-Nearest Neighbors: "Ask Your Neighbors"

The most intuitive classification algorithm there is, and today's running example -- not a dedicated future lecture, just a simple, honest way to build the mental model before Decision Trees, Logistic Regression, and SVM each bring their own approach.

The entire algorithm in one sentence: to classify a new point, find the k training points closest to it, and let them vote -- whichever category is most common among those neighbors is the prediction.

No training phase to speak of -- KNN just remembers the whole training set and does its "thinking" at prediction time, one new point at a time.

In [ 7 ]:
A New Way to Picture a Model

Decision Boundaries

Every classifier, once trained, draws an invisible line (or curve, or surface) through feature space -- everything on one side gets one label, everything on the other side gets the other. That line is the decision boundary, and it's the single most useful way to build intuition for how a classifier "thinks."

Tie straight back to Lecture 01: a wiggly, overly-complex boundary that curves around every single training point is overfitting -- the exact same picture as a high-degree polynomial, just drawn as a boundary instead of a curve. A boundary that's too simple to separate the classes at all is underfitting. The notebook shows this directly with KNN's `k`.

In [ 8 ]:
What's Actually Happening Under the Hood

Predictions Are Probabilities First, Labels Second

Most classifiers don't actually output "churn" or "stay" directly -- they output a probability, like "73% likely to churn," and only turn that into a hard label as a final, separate step.

.predict_proba()

Returns the probability of each class -- the raw, honest output of the model.

.predict()

Takes that probability and applies a cutoff (0.5 by default) to produce a hard label -- a convenience, not the whole story.

Why this distinction matters: a customer predicted at 51% churn risk and one predicted at 98% both get labeled "churn" by .predict() -- but they are very different situations. Anywhere the difference matters (prioritizing who to call first, deciding how much to trust a prediction), use the probability.

In [ 9 ]:
A Choice, Not a Law of Nature

The 0.5 Threshold Isn't Sacred

"Predict churn if probability > 0.5" is just scikit-learn's default -- it is not a rule handed down from anywhere. Moving that cutoff up or down changes the trade-off between two different kinds of mistakes, and the right cutoff depends entirely on which mistake costs more in your specific situation.

A concrete case: if missing a genuine churner (and losing them silently) is far more costly than an unnecessary retention email to someone who wasn't going to churn anyway, lowering the threshold below 0.5 -- flagging more people as "at risk" -- is the right business call, even though it looks like "lowering your standards" on paper.

The Foundation of Every Metric Today

The Confusion Matrix

Every classification metric from here on is just a different way of summarizing four numbers -- worth memorizing these four names cold, because everything else is built from them.

In [ 11 ]:
The Obvious Metric -- and Its Trap

Accuracy: "How Often Was I Right, Overall?"

Accuracy = (TP + TN) / (everything)

The percentage of predictions that were correct, full stop. Simple, intuitive -- and dangerously misleading the moment your classes are imbalanced.

The classic trap: if only 5% of customers churn, a model that predicts "never churns" for everyone is 95% accurate -- and catches zero actual churners. High accuracy, zero usefulness. This single example is worth remembering more than the formula.

In [ 12 ]:
A Metric That Doesn't Get Fooled by Imbalance

Precision: "When I Said Yes, Was I Right?"

Precision = TP / (TP + FP)

Out of everyone the model flagged as positive, what fraction actually were? High precision means few false alarms.

When precision matters most: when acting on a false positive is expensive or disruptive -- flagging a legitimate transaction as fraud and blocking a customer's card, or sending an aggressive retention offer to someone who was never going to leave.

In [ 13 ]:
The Other Half of the Story

Recall: "Of All the Real Yeses, How Many Did I Catch?"

Recall = TP / (TP + FN)

Out of everyone who actually was positive, what fraction did the model successfully catch? High recall means few misses.

When recall matters most: when missing a real positive is the costly mistake -- a disease that goes undiagnosed, fraud that slips through, a genuine churner nobody reaches out to before they leave.

In [ 14 ]:
Why You Can't Just Maximize Both

The Precision-Recall Tradeoff

Push the threshold down to catch more real positives (recall goes up), and you'll inevitably also flag more false alarms (precision goes down). Push it up to be more selective (precision goes up), and you'll inevitably miss more real cases (recall goes down). You can shift the balance -- you cannot usually improve both freely at once.

The extreme cases make this obvious: flag everyone as positive and recall hits 100% (you caught every real case) but precision craters (mostly false alarms). Flag no one and precision is undefined/perfect by default, but recall is 0%. The real decision always lives somewhere between these two extremes.

In [ 15 ]:
One Number That Balances Both

F1 Score

F1 = 2 × (Precision × Recall) / (Precision + Recall)

A single score that's high only when both precision and recall are reasonably high -- a model with great precision but terrible recall (or vice versa) still gets a low F1, unlike a plain average.

Why not just average them? A plain average of 100% precision and 0% recall would be 50% -- looking deceptively okay. F1 (a "harmonic mean") punishes that kind of lopsided result much more heavily, landing near 0% instead, which better reflects how useless that model actually is.

Use F1 as a good default single-number summary when you don't have a strong reason to favor precision or recall specifically.

In [ 16 ]:
Turning Theory Into a Decision

Choosing a Threshold Based on Real Costs

The best threshold isn't a math problem -- it's a business (or medical, or safety) question. Once you know roughly what a false positive costs versus what a false negative costs, the threshold that minimizes total cost can be found directly by trying different cutoffs and checking the confusion matrix at each one.

A simple way to think about it: if a missed churner costs you $200 in lost revenue, and an unnecessary retention offer costs $5, you should tolerate a lot of false positives to avoid false negatives -- the threshold should sit well below 0.5. The notebook works through this with real numbers.

In [ 17 ]:
The Big-Picture View

ROC Curve and AUC

Instead of picking one threshold and computing one confusion matrix, the ROC curve plots performance across every possible threshold at once -- true positive rate (recall) on one axis, false positive rate on the other.

AUC (Area Under the Curve) boils that whole curve down to one number, from 0.5 (no better than a coin flip) to 1.0 (perfect separation of the two classes). It answers "regardless of which threshold I eventually pick, how good is this model at ranking positives above negatives?" -- useful for comparing models before committing to any specific threshold.

Use ROC-AUC to compare models overall; use a specific threshold analysis (previous slide) to decide how to actually deploy the winner.

In [ 18 ]:
When the Default Approach Breaks

Class Imbalance

Fraud, disease, churn, defects -- most real "detect the rare bad thing" problems have wildly unequal classes, often 90/10 or worse. Every metric and every default setting needs a second look under these conditions.

The practical checklist: never trust accuracy alone (Slide 10); default to precision, recall, F1, and ROC-AUC instead; use StratifiedKFold for cross-validation (Lecture 04); and consider whether the model or the data itself needs adjusting, which the next slide covers.

In [ 19 ]:
Two Practical Fixes

Handling Imbalance: Reweighting and Resampling

Class weighting

class_weight='balanced' tells the model to penalize mistakes on the rare class more heavily during training -- no new data needed, just a different cost internally.

Resampling

Oversample the rare class (duplicate/synthesize more of it) or undersample the common class (use fewer of it) so training sees a more balanced picture.

Whichever you use, apply it only to training data -- exactly Lecture 04's rule. Resampling the test set would let you evaluate on an artificial distribution that doesn't reflect reality, which defeats the entire point of testing honestly.

In [ 20 ]:
Extending Beyond Two Classes

Multiclass Classification

Most of the algorithms in this course are naturally binary -- multiclass problems are usually solved by cleverly combining several binary classifiers under the hood.

One-vs-Rest (OvR)

Train one binary classifier per class: "class A vs. everything else," "class B vs. everything else," and so on. Whichever classifier is most confident wins.

One-vs-One (OvO)

Train one binary classifier for every pair of classes, then let them vote. More classifiers to train, but each one solves a simpler, more focused problem.

scikit-learn handles this automatically for most classifiers -- pass a multiclass target and specify the strategy if you want to override the default. Precision, recall, and F1 all extend naturally too: compute them per class, then average.

In [ 21 ]:
Bringing Lecture 04 Back

Comparing Classifiers Honestly

Nothing new here in mechanics -- cross_val_score, the same cv object for every candidate, StratifiedKFold for the fold strategy. What's new is which metric you hand it: scoring='f1' or 'roc_auc' instead of 'r2'.

One extra habit worth building: compare candidates on more than one metric side by side (accuracy, F1, ROC-AUC together) -- a model that wins on accuracy but loses badly on recall might be exactly the wrong choice for an imbalanced, high-stakes problem, and a single metric can hide that.
