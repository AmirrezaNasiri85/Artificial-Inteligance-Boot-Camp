Support Vector Machines

Where Logistic Regression asked "how do I maximize the likelihood of the labels?", SVM asks a completely different question: which straight line leaves the biggest possible gap between the two classes? That single shift in objective -- plus one very clever trick -- makes SVM one of the most powerful classifiers in this course.

Recap

Last Time, and Where This Sits

Lecture 09 gave Logistic Regression: a straight decision boundary found by maximizing likelihood -- searching for the (w, b) that makes the observed labels look as probable as possible. Today's algorithm draws a straight boundary too (at first), but gets there by optimizing something else entirely.

Worth saying out loud: both algorithms can only draw a straight line (until a kernel or feature-engineering trick enters the picture). What changes today is which straight line gets picked out of the infinitely many that could separate the data.

In [ 3 ]:
The Motivating Problem

Infinitely Many Lines Separate This Data

If two classes are cleanly separable, there isn't just one straight line that divides them -- there are infinitely many. Logistic Regression's maximum-likelihood objective picks one. But intuitively, some of those lines feel like a much safer choice than others.

The intuition: a line that barely squeezes between the classes is fragile -- a single new point close to the boundary could get misclassified. A line that sits as far as possible from both classes is safer. SVM turns that intuition into an exact optimization problem.

In [ 4 ]:
The Core Idea

The Margin: Maximizing the Gap

SVM doesn't just want a separating line -- it wants the line that is as far as possible from the nearest point of each class. That distance, doubled, is called the margin.

Formally: among every line that separates the classes, SVM picks the one whose distance to the nearest point of either class is as large as possible. This is called the maximum-margin classifier.

In [ 5 ]:
Where the Name Comes From

Support Vectors: the Points That Matter

Look again at the diagram from the last slide: only the three circled points actually touch the margin boundary. Those are the support vectors -- and they are the only points that determine where the line and the margin sit.

What this means

Every other point could be moved around, added, or deleted -- as long as it stays outside the margin -- and the decision boundary would not change at all. The model is entirely "supported" by a small subset of the training data.

Why it matters

This is a real structural difference from Logistic Regression, whose coefficients depend at least a little on every training point. SVM's solution is sparse -- often only a handful of points out of thousands actually matter.

In scikit-learn: after fitting an SVC, model.support_vectors_ gives you exactly these points, and model.n_support_ tells you how many support vectors were needed per class -- often a small fraction of the training set.

In [ 6 ]:
Turning the Picture Into Math

The Hard-Margin SVM

The decision boundary is still the familiar linear form z = w·x + b = 0. What's new is the constraint: every training point must sit on the correct side and at least a fixed distance away.

minimize ½‖w‖²  subject to  yi(w·xi + b) ≥ 1 for every point i
  • Minimizing ‖w‖ is the same thing as maximizing the margin width -- smaller w means a wider gap between the boundary and the constraint planes.
  • The constraint yi(w·xi+b) ≥ 1 says: every point of class +1 must land at z ≥ 1, every point of class -1 at z ≤ -1 -- correctly classified, with room to spare.
  • This is called the hard margin because no point is allowed to violate it -- it only works when the classes are perfectly separable.

No calculus needed to use this well: this is a constrained optimization problem solved by the library, not by hand -- the takeaway is the objective's shape (small weights, wide margin), not the solver mechanics.

In [ 7 ]:
When Real Data Doesn't Cooperate

Soft Margin: Allowing a Few Violations

Real data is almost never perfectly separable -- there's usually noise, overlap, or a mislabeled point or two. A hard margin would have no solution at all in that case. The fix: let some points violate the margin, but penalize how badly.

minimize ½‖w‖² + C ∑ ξi

Each point gets a slack variable ξi ≥ 0 measuring how far it strays onto the wrong side of its margin (zero if it's fine). The objective now trades off two competing goals: a wide margin, and few/small violations. C controls that trade-off -- more on it in a few slides.

This is the version scikit-learn actually fits. Setting C very large pushes it to behave almost like a hard margin; smaller C tolerates more slack in exchange for a wider, more robust margin.

In [ 8 ]:
The Cost Function, Named

Hinge Loss

The slack-variable formulation from the last slide is equivalent to minimizing a specific cost function called hinge loss, applied to each point plus the same ‖w‖² regularization term.

cost(z, y) = max(0, 1 − y·z)
  • Correctly classified with margin to spare (y·z ≥ 1) → cost is exactly zero. Log loss from Lecture 09 never reaches zero; hinge loss does, once a point is safely past the margin.
  • Inside the margin or on the wrong side → cost grows linearly, not exponentially the way log loss's confident-wrong penalty does.
  • This zero-cost region for "already correct enough" predictions is exactly what produces the sparse support-vector solution from two slides ago -- points that aren't near the boundary contribute nothing to the loss at all.

Compare to Lecture 09: log loss punishes confident wrong predictions increasingly harshly and never lets any point "stop mattering." Hinge loss is more forgiving once a point clears the margin -- a genuinely different philosophy about what training should optimize.

In [ 9 ]:
Controlling the Trade-off

The C Parameter, Once More

SVM's C plays a similar role to Logistic Regression's C -- but the direction of the trade-off is worth stating carefully in its own terms.

Small C

Violations are cheap. The optimizer prioritizes a wide margin even if that means misclassifying or cutting close to a few points. Smoother boundary, more tolerant of noise, higher bias.

Large C

Violations are expensive. The optimizer prioritizes getting every point right, even if the margin has to narrow to do it. Tighter fit to training data, lower bias, higher variance.

Same instinct as Lecture 09's regularization: this is still a bias-variance knob, and it still gets tuned with GridSearchCV rather than guessed. The mechanism is different (margin width vs. coefficient shrinkage), but the underlying trade-off -- simpler vs. more flexible -- is the same idea in new clothes.

In [ 10 ]:
A Familiar Wall

But It's Still Just a Straight Line

Everything so far -- hard margin, soft margin, hinge loss, C -- still produces a single straight decision boundary. If the true boundary between classes genuinely curves, none of this helps, exactly like Logistic Regression's limitation from last lecture.

Lecture 09's fix was to manually engineer curved features and feed them into the same linear model. SVM has a far more elegant way to get the same effect -- without ever explicitly constructing those extra features.

In [ 11 ]:
The Signature Idea

The Kernel Trick

Lift the data into a higher-dimensional space where it does become linearly separable, fit a straight (hyper)plane there, then map that boundary back down. The "trick" is that the math never actually has to build the higher-dimensional points -- it only ever needs the dot products between them.

The trick, precisely: the SVM optimization only ever needs xi · xj between pairs of points, never the raw feature vectors. A kernel function K(xi, xj) computes what that dot product would have been in the lifted space directly from the original points -- skipping the (possibly infinite-dimensional) lift entirely.

In [ 12 ]:
Choosing the Lift

Common Kernels

scikit-learn's SVC(kernel=...) ships with a handful of standard choices, each implying a different "shape" of decision boundary back in the original space.

KernelFormulaBoundary shape
linearK(x,x') = x·x'Straight line / flat plane -- no lift at all
polyK(x,x') = (γx·x' + r)dCurved, polynomial-shaped boundaries
rbf (Gaussian)K(x,x') = exp(−γ‖x−x'‖²)Smooth, flexible "blob-like" boundaries -- the default
sigmoidK(x,x') = tanh(γx·x' + r)Behaves similarly to a small neural network

RBF is the default in scikit-learn and a strong first choice for most problems -- it's implicitly lifting into an infinite-dimensional space, yet the kernel trick still makes it computationally feasible.

In [ 13 ]:
The RBF Kernel's Own Knob

The gamma Parameter

For the rbf and poly kernels, gamma controls how far a single training point's "influence" reaches -- a second hyperparameter that shapes the boundary alongside C.

Small gamma

Each point's influence reaches far. Boundaries are smooth and close to linear. Risk of underfitting if too small.

Large gamma

Each point's influence is tightly local. Boundaries can wrap closely around individual points -- powerful, but a classic overfitting risk.

Two knobs, one search: C and gamma interact, so scikit-learn practice is to search both together in a single GridSearchCV over a 2D grid, not tune them one at a time.

In [ 14 ]:
Beyond Two Classes

Multiclass SVM

Unlike Logistic Regression's native softmax, SVM has no single joint multiclass formulation in general use -- it combines several binary SVMs instead, using one of two standard strategies.

One-vs-Rest (OvR)

Train one SVM per class: "this class" vs. "everything else." For k classes, that's k binary SVMs. Predict by picking the class whose SVM is most confident.

One-vs-One (OvO)

Train one SVM per pair of classes -- k(k−1)/2 of them. Predict by a majority vote across all pairwise classifiers. scikit-learn's SVC uses this by default.

In practice: sklearn.svm.SVC handles the OvO combination automatically behind the scenes -- calling .fit() on multiclass labels just works, the same way it did for LogisticRegression in Lecture 09.

The Honest Trade-offs

Strengths and Weaknesses

Strengths

  • Effective in high-dimensional spaces, even when features outnumber samples
  • Kernel trick gives access to very flexible, curved boundaries without hand-engineering features
  • Sparse solution -- only support vectors matter, which can aid memory and interpretability of which points drive the model
  • Strong out-of-the-box performance on small-to-medium, cleanly-separable-ish datasets

Weaknesses

  • No native probability output -- predict_proba requires an extra, somewhat costly calibration step
  • Sensitive to feature scaling, just like every distance/margin-based method so far
  • Slow to train on large datasets -- training time scales poorly (often worse than quadratically) with the number of samples
  • Two interacting hyperparameters (C, gamma) to tune instead of one

Neural Networks and the Perceptron

SVM found one clever way to bend a linear method into something more flexible via the kernel trick. Neural networks take a different route entirely: stacking many simple linear-plus-nonlinearity units together, letting the network itself learn which curved shapes it needs.
